Overview

This master's thesis presents the design, implementation, and evaluation of a perception payload for autonomous small-UAS navigation in structured, GNSS-denied environments such as maintenance tunnels and indoor corridors. The system integrates depth imagery, Short-Wave Infrared (SWIR) imagery, and Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) thermal imaging with an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) to simultaneously estimate the platform's 6-DOF pose and generate a thermal anomaly map of the environment.

Motivation

Small-size UAS (sUAS) offer unique access to hazardous and confined environments — such as underground steam tunnels and industrial maintenance shafts — where GNSS signals are entirely unavailable and human inspection is risky. Achieving reliable autonomous navigation in these unstructured, GPS-denied spaces requires a carefully designed perception payload and a computationally efficient navigation algorithm capable of running in real time on a low-power embedded processor.

Platform and Sensors

The perception payload was integrated on a Blackout quadcopter equipped with:

  • Flight Controller: 3DR Pixhawk running PX4
  • Onboard Computer: Odroid XU4 running Ubuntu + ROS
  • 3D Depth Sensor: Occipital Structure Sensor — provides both SWIR imagery and dense 3D point clouds (FOV: 58° H × 45° V; range: 0.4–3.5 m)
  • Thermal Camera: FLIR Lepton LWIR camera — captures 14-bit thermal images via SPI; detects heat signatures of steam leaks, exposed pipes, and insulation failures
  • IMU: Pixhawk onboard MEMS IMU — provides high-rate accelerations and angular rates

All sensors were interfaced and time-synchronized via ROS on the Odroid XU4.

Key Contributions

1. Multi-Sensor Calibration

A rigorous calibration pipeline was developed covering intrinsic calibration (lens distortion, focal length) for both the Occipital and Lepton cameras, extrinsic calibration (rotation and translation) between the two imaging sensors using a shared asymmetric circle grid target, and temporal calibration between the IMU and the point cloud stream using cross-correlation of orientation signals. Calibration results showed sub-degree rotation error between the cameras.

2. Point Cloud Segmentation and Planar Feature Extraction

A RANSAC-based planar surface extraction algorithm was developed to identify floor, ceiling, and wall planes from the incoming 3D point cloud. The algorithm uses environment slicing — partitioning the point cloud into horizontal z-slices — to decompose the scene into 2D cross-sections from which lines, points, and arc features are extracted and combined into 3D planar surfaces. The algorithm runs in real time at 3 Hz on the Odroid XU4 (40–80 ms per frame).

3. LWIR Thermal Anomaly Detection

A thermal feature extraction pipeline was implemented to automatically detect hot pipes, steam leaks, and insulation failures from LWIR imagery. The approach uses dynamic histogram-based thresholding (normalizing for ambient temperature variation) and edge extraction to isolate thermally significant objects. Detected anomalies are projected into the 3D map for geo-referenced inspection reporting. Results from Ohio University's steam tunnels successfully identified hot pipes and a steam leak with condensed water on the floor.

4. Navigation Architecture

The hierarchical Guidance, Navigation and Control (GNC) framework integrates the planar surface features from the 3D imager with the IMU in a Kalman filter for 6-DOF pose estimation. A Complementary Kalman Filter (CKF) drives the dynamics estimator, and an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) handles feature-based position updates. The planar and point feature observations constrain translation and heading drift during GPS-denied flight.

Experimental Results

The platform was flight-tested in Ohio University's underground maintenance steam tunnels and in Stocker Center hallways. Key results include:

  • Successful extraction of floor, ceiling, and wall planes across three distinct indoor environments (maintenance tunnel, straight corridor, inclined-wall corridor)
  • Reliable thermal anomaly detection including hot steam pipes, exposed insulation, and a steam leak with floor condensation
  • Temporal calibration between IMU and point cloud confirmed sub-10 ms latency across five independent runs
  • Real-time pre-processing at 3 Hz on Odroid XU4 with O2 optimization (70–80 ms/frame)
  • Accurate extrinsic calibration between SWIR and LWIR cameras — residual scaling error within acceptable bounds for the sensor's 0.4–3.5 m operating range
